## The problem

**In the age of large foundation models, why bother with personalization?**

Generic vision-language models are trained on images pulled from the internet, and they do not
perform as well on photos taken by blind and low vision users. Under- and over-exposure, atypical
framing, objects half out of shot: the data does not match what the model expects. GPT-4V will
confidently misread a thermometer that a blind user has photographed perfectly reasonably.

There is a second gap that scale does not close. Even a flawless generic recogniser identifies *a*
set of keys — the category. It cannot identify *your* keys, your pill bottle, your folding guide
cane. And if you are blind, the category is rarely what you need. You already know what keys are.
You need to know where yours went.

**Teachable AI** answers both: users provide their own examples and personalise the model themselves
through few-shot adaptation, then teach, test, adjust and repeat until it performs as they need.

<Figure
  src={`${M}/teachable-ai-diagram.png`}
  alt="A loop diagram of a teachable AI system: the user provides training examples, the system trains, the user tests it, and the cycle repeats until it performs as desired."
  caption="In a teachable AI system, the user provides training examples and iterates until the system performs as desired."
  wide
/>

That is the paradigm. The rest of this is the interaction design problem: a teaching loop only works
if a person can actually close it.

A feature that helps people who are blind or low vision locate their personal objects.

## Challenge 1 — The best model on paper was the wrong model

We evaluated three feature extractors. On average frame accuracy, the winner was obvious:

| Feature extractor | Pre-training | Parameters | Avg. frame accuracy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EfficientNetB0 | ImageNet1K (supervised) | 4.01M | 69.8% |
| EfficientNetV2 | ImageNet21K (supervised) | 20.18M | 73.8% |
| ViT-B-32 (CLIP) | LAION2B (self-supervised) | 87.46M | **74.8%** |

Then we put them on phones. The two larger models took significantly longer to personalise and to
run inference. CLIP was almost unusable on older iPhones.

<Figure
  src={`${M}/model-speed-on-device.png`}
  alt="A chart comparing on-device speed: EfficientNetB0 personalises and runs inference far faster than EfficientNetV2 and CLIP, with CLIP the slowest by a wide margin."
  caption="Ported to device, the larger models were far slower to personalise and to run inference."
  wide
/>

Speed mattered for reasons that have nothing to do with benchmarks.

**The model will fail, and it has to fail gracefully.** Recognition is sometimes wrong. When it is,
the user needs to clear the false lead and move on quickly — which makes inference speed part of the
error experience, not a performance stat.

<MediaGrid cols={2} colsMobile={1} wide caption="Helping the model fail gracefully by enabling a fast feedback loop.">
  <Figure
    src={`${M}/fail-gracefully-01.png`}
    alt="A user encountering an incorrect recognition and quickly dismissing the false lead."
  />
  <Figure
    src={`${M}/fail-gracefully-02.png`}
    alt="The user re-recording and trying again moments later, because the loop is fast."
  />
</MediaGrid>

**Users test immediately, in context.** People taught an object and tried it straight away, on their
own carpet, on their own couch. When it failed they formed theories — *maybe my glasses are hard to
spot on a glass table because they are reflective* — re-recorded, and tried again. That loop is
where personalisation actually happens, and its speed is set by how fast the model personalises.

**We shipped EfficientNetB0 — the least accurate model.** Five percentage points of frame accuracy
traded for a model 20x smaller, because personalisation and inference speed are what let a person
close the loop, and closing the loop is what gets them to their keys.

> Going beyond aggregate metrics is not a philosophical point. It changes which model ships.

## Challenge 2 — Users had no mental model of how a camera works

In the user study we watched people position the phone in ways no sighted user would: aiming at the
floor when they thought the object was on the floor, at a chair when they thought it was on a chair,
holding it flat when they meant to survey the whole room.

    caption="When they think the object is on the chair"

Entirely logical, and not how cameras work. Many of our users, especially those born blind, had
never needed a working model of a camera's field of view. Nothing in their experience makes it
obvious that a camera sees a cone, or that surveying a room means a slow pan rather than pointing at
a place.

**This is the failure mode no amount of model quality fixes.** However good recognition gets, if a
person cannot aim the camera, they cannot find their object.

**The fix was onboarding we had assumed we would not need — and metaphor did most of the work.** The
camera is *a pair of eyes*. The audio cues are *a metal detector*. Both import understanding people
already have, and both carry the right implications: eyes point somewhere and have a field of view;
a metal detector gets louder as you get closer. We built a tutorial teaching the physical technique,
like panning the camera slowly.

## Challenge 3 — The last mile

Our first audio design keyed feedback to whether the object was **in view of the camera**.

Then we watched a user struggle to find his keys when they were already at his fingertips. At close
range the camera had lost sight of them, so the app went quiet — exactly when he was closest. The
system was reporting the camera's state when he needed the object's location.

<Figure
  src={`${M}/last-mile-problem.png`}
  alt="A user reaching toward keys that are right in front of him while the app has gone silent, because the camera has lost sight of them at close range."
  caption="Over-reliance on having the object in view was confusing for users."
  wide
/>

**The fix was to make guidance depend on the object's 3D location rather than its presence in
frame.** Once localised, the object's position persists, so guidance keeps working as the phone
moves and the object drifts out of shot.

The metaphor was a **rubber band**: a continuous pull toward the object that tightens as you close
in. Tension you can feel, rather than a signal that cuts out at the moment of success.

  cols={2}

## Results

**83% success rate** — the app helped users find their objects in 71 of 86 uses. **12 of 15
participants** felt more confident finding their things.

Most telling for the personalisation argument: participants taught **58 objects, forming 37 visually
distinct clusters**. Some were common — keys (7), earphones (3), pill bottles (3). The long tail was
single instances: a measuring tape, a plastic scrunchy, pliers, a pot, a purse. One user each.

## What I would take to the next project

1. **Aggregate metrics can select the wrong model.** Frame accuracy said CLIP. The experience said
   EfficientNetB0 — five points worse and 20x smaller. Ask what the metric stands in for, then check
   whether it still holds on the actual device, inside the actual loop.
2. **A model is only as good as the system it underpins.** A world-class model inside a system that
   does not fit the user is a world-class model nobody can use.
3. **Check what users already believe about the technology.** We designed for people who could not
   see the object. We had not designed for people who had never needed a mental model of a camera.
4. **Metaphor is an interface.** *Pair of eyes* and *metal detector* did more onboarding work than
   any instruction we wrote, because they import understanding people already have.
5. **Design the last mile separately.** The endgame of a guidance system has its own failure modes.
   Ours broke precisely at the moment of success.
